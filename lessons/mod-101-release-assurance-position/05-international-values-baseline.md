# International Values Baseline — OECD, Council of Europe, UNESCO

## Motivation

The NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, and the EU AI Act each carry technical and procedural obligations. Beneath all three sits a shorter, older layer of *values instruments* — the OECD AI Principles, the Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI, and the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI.

These instruments are not technical standards, and (with the exception of the Framework Convention, which is a treaty for states that ratify it) they are not directly enforceable against a company's release-gate. What they *are* is the international-consensus values baseline that every technical framework, standard, and regulation above them is intended to be consistent with. A release-assurance program that discharges its EU AI Act obligations but produces a system that visibly undercuts, say, the UNESCO Recommendation's principles of proportionality or human oversight will (a) draw regulator attention downstream and (b) fail to satisfy the enterprise's own responsible-AI narrative.

For a release-assurance owner, the practical use is simple: **the values baseline is the check you run against the assurance case to catch obligations the technical frameworks alone would let you miss.** This chapter reads all three instruments in outline and shows how the release-assurance program uses them.

## OECD AI Principles

**Adopted 2019, updated 2024.** The OECD Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence — the OECD AI Principles — was the first intergovernmental values instrument on AI and has been endorsed by 47+ jurisdictions. It is the anchor most other instruments cross-reference.

### Values-based principles

- **Inclusive growth, sustainable development, and well-being.** AI should benefit people and the planet.
- **Human rights, democratic values, including fairness and privacy.** AI systems should respect the rule of law, human rights, and democratic values throughout their lifecycle.
- **Transparency and explainability.** People should understand AI-based outcomes and be able to challenge them.
- **Robustness, security, and safety.** AI systems should be robust, secure, and safe throughout their lifecycle; risks should be continually assessed and managed.
- **Accountability.** AI actors should be accountable for the proper functioning of AI systems and for the respect of the above principles.

### Recommendations for policymakers

- Invest in AI research and development.
- Foster a digital ecosystem for AI.
- Shape an enabling policy environment.
- Build human capacity and prepare for labour-market transformation.
- Engage international cooperation.

### Release-assurance implication

The OECD principles are the shortest and most stable of the three values instruments and are the ones that the release-assurance program's assurance-case top-level claims should always be readable against. If the assurance case cannot say "yes, this system's release respects human rights and democratic values" or "yes, we have accountability for its proper functioning," the technical evidence is not going to save it.

The OECD also publishes an **AI Classification Framework** that many programs use to categorise systems by context, data and input, model, task and output, and impacts — useful as an intake-time device (owned upstream by the analyst), and useful as a shared vocabulary in the release-gate scope statement (`mod-103`).

## Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI

**Opened for signature 2024-09-05.** The Council of Europe's *Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law* is the first *binding international treaty* on AI. States that sign and ratify take on treaty obligations to secure the Convention's principles for AI systems within their jurisdiction.

The Convention applies to AI systems used by public authorities and, subject to each Party's choice of implementation, private actors carrying out relevant activities.

### Key principles (Chapter II)

- **Human dignity and individual autonomy.**
- **Equality and non-discrimination**, including gender equality.
- **Respect for privacy and personal data protection.**
- **Transparency and oversight**, including identification of content generated by AI systems where relevant.
- **Accountability and responsibility** for adverse impacts.
- **Reliability**, including robustness, cybersecurity, and functional safety.
- **Safe innovation**, including controlled testing environments (regulatory sandboxes).

### Procedural principles

- **Documentation and information**, so people affected can understand and, where relevant, challenge AI-based decisions.
- **Effective remedies** for adverse impacts.
- **Procedural safeguards** for people subject to AI-based decisions.
- **Risk and impact management framework**, including assessment and mitigation of risks and impacts on human rights, democracy, and the rule of law.

### Release-assurance implication

Two things matter for a release-gate owner.

First, in jurisdictions that have ratified the Convention, the values it lists inform how domestic AI legislation is drafted and how domestic regulators interpret existing law. A release-gate whose evidence supports the Convention's principles will be easier to defend in national proceedings under whatever specific domestic instrument applies.

Second, the Convention's *procedural* principles (documentation, effective remedies, procedural safeguards) are non-obvious release-gate criteria. Many technical release-gates cover reliability and cybersecurity but under-cover the availability of effective remedies for affected people. The values baseline is where the release-assurance program catches that gap.

## UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence

**Adopted 2021-11.** The UNESCO Recommendation is the most widely subscribed values instrument on AI — endorsed by 193 UNESCO Member States. It is a soft-law recommendation rather than a treaty, but it is the reference document many countries cite when building national AI ethics frameworks.

### Values (Chapter II)

- **Respect, protection, and promotion of human rights and fundamental freedoms and human dignity.**
- **Environment and ecosystem flourishing.**
- **Ensuring diversity and inclusiveness.**
- **Living in peaceful, just, and interconnected societies.**

### Principles (Chapter III)

- **Proportionality and do no harm.**
- **Safety and security.**
- **Fairness and non-discrimination.**
- **Sustainability.**
- **Right to privacy and data protection.**
- **Human oversight and determination.**
- **Transparency and explainability.**
- **Responsibility and accountability.**
- **Awareness and literacy.**
- **Multi-stakeholder and adaptive governance and collaboration.**

### Policy Action Areas (Chapter IV)

The Recommendation lists eleven policy action areas that Member States should implement — impact assessment, governance and stewardship, data policy, development and international cooperation, environment and ecosystems, gender, culture, education and research, communication and information, economy and labour, and health and social well-being. Two of these are directly relevant to release-assurance:

- **Ethical impact assessment.** The Recommendation calls for AI ethical impact assessments; UNESCO's Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM) and Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA) tool operationalise them.
- **Governance and stewardship.** The Recommendation calls for oversight mechanisms — the assurance program is one of them.

### Release-assurance implication

The UNESCO Recommendation's principles overlap heavily with the OECD AI Principles and the Framework Convention on the core ethical vocabulary. Two principles distinctive to the release-gate:

- **Proportionality and do no harm.** The measures chosen should not exceed what is necessary to achieve the legitimate aim. This is the values-baseline check on release-gates that lean over-strict or over-lax for the risk they carry.
- **Sustainability.** Environmental and social sustainability appears in the Recommendation more explicitly than in the OECD Principles. A release-gate for a large model may reasonably attach evidence on environmental impact (energy, water, embodied carbon) as evidence for this principle.

## Using the values baseline in practice

The values baseline is not a separate release-gate. It is a *cross-check* the release-assurance program runs against the assurance case. The pattern:

1. **After** the technical release-gate evidence has been assembled and the assurance case drafted, walk each top-level claim against the OECD principles, the Framework Convention principles, and the UNESCO principles.
2. For each principle, ask: *does the release-gate evidence, as a whole, support this principle? Or does the system's release visibly undercut it?*
3. For any principle where the answer is not "yes, supported," add a claim to the assurance case with corresponding evidence. Common finds: sustainability (environmental impact evidence missing), effective remedies (no channel for affected people to appeal), transparency to end-users (system card exists for auditors but not for end-users), proportionality (release-gate over-collects data for a low-risk deployment).
4. Where a principle cannot be discharged inside the release-gate — e.g. effective remedies in a jurisdiction without the legal instrument to support them — record the residual as a program-level risk and escalate upward to the architect and head.

## Summary

- OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024), Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (2024), and UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) form an international consensus values baseline for AI.
- OECD principles: inclusive growth, human rights, transparency, robustness / security / safety, accountability.
- Framework Convention principles: human dignity, equality, privacy, transparency, accountability, reliability, safe innovation — plus procedural safeguards, documentation, and effective remedies.
- UNESCO principles: proportionality and do no harm, safety, fairness, sustainability, privacy, human oversight, transparency, responsibility, awareness, multi-stakeholder governance.
- The release-assurance program uses the values baseline as a cross-check on the assurance case, not as a separate gate. It catches obligations (effective remedies, sustainability, proportionality) that the technical frameworks alone would let you miss.
